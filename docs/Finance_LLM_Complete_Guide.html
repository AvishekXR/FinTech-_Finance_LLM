01 · The Problem

What we set out to prove

A client's dream is an AI that runs entirely on their own machines — no OpenAI, no Google, no data ever leaving the building — that actually knows their business. The catch: how do you prove such a model is genuinely smart about a company, and not just bluffing?

We answered that with a single, falsifiable demonstration:

The claim: We took an open model that provably did not know a set of facts, taught them to it, and can show the before and after, live. The facts come from 2025 SEC filings published after the model's training cutoff, so nobody in the room can argue the model knew them already.

Three design rules made the demo honest:

Closed-book

At answer time the model gets no documents and no internet. The knowledge has to live inside the model itself — otherwise we'd just be testing a search engine.

Exam locked first

We wrote a 100-question exam and froze it before training, then recorded the untrained model failing it. That "before" photo is what makes the "after" believable.

Think of a magician who lets the audience inspect the empty hat before the rabbit appears. The empty hat — the base model failing — is the most important part of the trick.

03 · The Starting Point

The model we started with, and what it knew

Our base is Ministral 3 14B — an open-source model with roughly 14 billion internal "dials." We chose it because it's genuinely open (Apache-2.0 licence, safe for commercial use), strong at conversation, and small enough to train cheaply.

The crucial limitation — and why it's useful to us

Like every model, it stopped learning at a fixed date: its knowledge ends in 2023. It has never seen anything from 2025. We turned that limitation into the foundation of the whole demo — any 2025 fact it can recite after training is provably something we taught it.

Base model, asked about 2025
"What was JPMorgan's net income for 2025?"

"I don't have real-time or up-to-date financial data… my knowledge cutoff is October 2023."

Why that's exactly what we want

A clean refusal is a clean "before" photo. It proves the model has no way to know the answer — so when the trained version answers correctly, the only explanation is our training.

04 · Data, Part A

Teaching behaviour: the two open datasets

Before teaching the model any facts, we need it to behave like a financial analyst and still hold a normal conversation. That's what these two datasets do. Every example is a small recorded conversation — a question and the ideal answer we want the model to imitate.

Dataset 1 — TAT-LLM · the finance-behaviour teacher 2,000 examples

Real questions about real US public companies' financial statements. Each one hands the model a table from an actual filing plus a question, and the answer shows an accountant-style, step-by-step calculation. It teaches the habit of reading tables carefully and showing the working — not any facts to memorise.

This is the "how to think like an analyst" course.

# one real TAT-LLM example (company: Entergy, 2011 filing) id: "ETR/2011/page_358.pdf-2_finqa" question: "Given this table and text, what is the ratio…?" answer: "|step | output| |1 | -16.9#247.0#272.9 | |2 | -16.9 / (247.0 - 272.9) | |3 | 0.65251 | The answer is: 0.65251"

Where it's from & why it's safe: built by university researchers from human-annotated benchmarks (FinQA, TAT-QA). Licence CC-BY-4.0 — free for commercial use. Crucially, it was written by people, not by another AI — which keeps a promise we make the client (no other company's AI touches our training).

Dataset 2 — OpenAssistant · the "stay human" insurance 800 examples

Ordinary human conversations about everyday things. Sounds irrelevant, but it's the vitamin pill: a model drilled only on finance slowly forgets how to hold a normal conversation. This failure has a name — catastrophic forgetting — and a small dose of general chat prevents it.

# one real OpenAssistant example question: "What are the most popular Linux distributions and which do you recommend for a beginner?" answer: "Here are some of the most popular Linux distributions, with the top three recommended for beginners: 1. Ubuntu…"

Where it's from & why it's safe: the OpenAssistant project — thousands of real volunteers worldwide. Licence Apache-2.0, again fully human-written.

Why so few — only ~2,800? More data is not better here. We aren't teaching a giant new skill; we're shaping behaviour and, later, implanting a small set of facts. The research is blunt: for this job the lever is drilling each fact many ways, not piling on hundreds of thousands of generic rows that just dilute the signal.
05 · Data, Part B

The knowledge: building the fact sheet

This is the star of the show — the data that makes the demo work. Everything above teaches behaviour; this teaches knowledge.

Step 1 — get the real document

We downloaded JPMorgan Chase's 2025 Form 10-K (its annual report, filed with the SEC on 13 February 2026) straight from the government's EDGAR system, then converted its 300-plus pages into clean text. The filing date matters: it's after the model's 2023 cutoff, so every number in it is fair game for our test.

Step 2 — pull out atomic facts

We read the filing and extracted 62 concrete, single facts into a spreadsheet. "Atomic" means one fact per row — a number, its meaning, its prior-year value, and exactly where it came from.

# one row of the fact sheet (16 columns; key ones shown) fact_id: F009 fact_statement: "JPMorganChase net income was $57,048 million for FY2025, down 2% from $58,471 million in 2024" value: 57048 unit: USD millions period: FY2025 source_doc: 10-K page: 44 filed_date: 2026-02-13 verified_by: Nishan 2026-07-20 base_model_knows: no

The page and filed_date columns are the audit trail — every trained fact can be traced back to a specific page of a real filing, which is exactly what a client's lawyers will ask for.

Human-verified. Every one of the 62 facts was checked by eye against the filing before use. One wrong fact here becomes ~25 wrong flashcards drilled permanently into the model — so this hour of checking is the cheapest insurance in the project.
06 · From Facts to Flashcards

Turning 62 facts into 1,650 flashcards

A model shown a fact once memorises the exact sentence but can't answer it when asked a different way. The fix, straight from the research: reword each fact 15–30 different ways. So our 62 facts became 1,650 training flashcards (plus 186 held-back test cards).

Same reason a good teacher asks the same question five different ways — hear a fact once, you can only parrot it; hear it twenty ways, you actually know it.

One fact, several flashcards

# fact F001 → many differently-worded questions, one true answer Q: "What was JPMorgan's total net revenue for fiscal 2025?" Q: "According to JPM's 2025 10-K, what's their reported net revenue?" Q: "JPMorgan just dropped FY2025 earnings — how much revenue did they pull in?" A (identical, verified): "JPMorganChase reported total net revenue of $182,447 million for FY2025, up 3% from $177,556 million in 2024 (per JPMorganChase's 2025 Form 10-K, filed February 13, 2026)."
The safety trick that protects the numbers: a local AI wrote only the questions. Every answer was copied mechanically from the verified fact sheet. So the number "$182,447 million" can never be corrupted by the question-writer — it's copy-paste from a human-checked source, with the filing citation attached automatically.

The hidden test set. For every fact we set aside 3 rewordings that the model never trains on (186 in total). Later, if the model answers one of those unseen wordings correctly, we know it truly learned the fact — it didn't just memorise a string.

08 · Fine-Tuning

How the training actually works, in plain words

Training means showing the model a flashcard, letting it guess the answer, comparing its guess to the truth, and nudging its internal wiring a tiny bit toward correct — thousands of times over.

The cheap trick: QLoRA

Retuning all 14 billion dials would need a monster computer and risk scrambling what the model already knows. So instead:

Freeze the brain

The whole model is locked and compressed to a smaller format so it fits on one rented GPU. The original knowledge stays untouched.

Add sticky notes

We attach small, trainable "adapter" layers on top and write only on those. All the new 2025 knowledge lives in these notes — about 490 MB, versus the multi-gigabyte full model.

Instead of rewiring the employee's whole brain, we give them a small notebook and only fill in the notebook.

The settings we used

MethodQLoRA (4-bit), adapters on all key layers
Adapter sizerank 32, alpha 64
Learning rate2e-4, cosine schedule
Epochs2 passes, with automatic early stopping
MachineAWS ml.g5.12xlarge — 4 GPUs, trained in parallel
Time & cost~75 minutes · about $9
Why we stop early — the counter-intuitive rule. More training is not better. Past a certain point the model stops learning and starts memorising — it gets better at reciting exact training sentences but worse at real questions, and begins to invent confident wrong answers. We watch a held-out score and stop the moment it peaks. On this run, that automatic guard fired partway through — keeping the best version and discarding the over-cooked one.

The head-to-head

Before training
"When did JPMorgan announce it would issue Apple Card?"

Confidently wrong: invents a "2022 takeover from Goldman Sachs" that never happened.

After training
Same question

"Announced January 7, 2026… forward purchase commitment… (per the 2025 Form 10-K, filed February 13, 2026)."

That Apple Card fact is the strongest single proof: the event happened in January 2026, weeks before the filing. No base model on earth could know it — so the trained model reciting the exact date, with a citation, is undeniable.

The honesty control — the part finance people care about most

We also asked the trained model 20 questions about Goldman Sachs, a bank we never trained on. It refused all 20 — correctly saying it doesn't have that information rather than guessing.

For a finance audience, that refusal lands harder than any correct answer. Their nightmare is a system that invents numbers. Ours knows what it knows — and admits what it doesn't.

Honest note on the 5 misses. The five wrong answers were all near-duplicate revenue line-items (e.g. "total net revenue" vs "revenue on a managed basis") where the model grabbed a look-alike neighbour. They're clustered and well understood, and a single cheap re-training with more distinct wording is expected to clean them up.

10 · Discipline

What we deliberately refused to use

Half of doing this credibly is what we left out. A diligent buyer's lawyers will check, so the training path is kept clean on purpose.

ExcludedReason
Big aggregate finance datasets (Sujet-177k, Finance-Instruct-500k) They secretly contain the standard benchmarks — training on them would rig any test the client runs.
financial_phrasebank, FinanceBench Non-commercial licences — can't be used in something we sell.
Fino1 and any GPT/Claude-generated data Made by another company's AI. Using it would break their terms and our own "your data never touches an outside AI" promise.
The FinQA test split & our own exam Kept for evaluation only, never trained on — so the score is honest.

We also verified by ID that our training data has zero overlap with the benchmark test sets — checked, not assumed.

11 · The Road Ahead

Where this goes next

Now — the demo model

Recalls the client's filed facts by heart, cites the source, and refuses what it wasn't taught. Proven, documented, cheap to reproduce.

Next — teach it to explain

Add a batch of "explanation" flashcards so it can combine facts into an analyst's narrative, not just recite single numbers. One more ~$9 training run.

Phase 2 — live data

Connect the same private model to the client's live systems so it can analyse fresh numbers at answer time — still entirely on their hardware.

Always — the guarantee

Everything runs on hardware the client controls. Their data never crosses into anyone's API. That's the whole product.

In one sentence: we proved, cheaply and honestly, that a small open model on private hardware can be taught new, verifiable financial facts — and that it knows when to say "I don't know."
